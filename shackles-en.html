Text: “The Shackles”

Death Row Block, Huaiyang Prefecture Correctional Facility

After morning roll call, Zhang opened Records of the Grand Historian, borrowed from the correctional facility library. He had begun reading the “Treatise on the Xiongnu” a few days earlier, but progress was slow. There were many unfamiliar characters, and he had to read each sentence repeatedly just to grasp its meaning.

The iron door of his solitary cell opened at an unusual hour.

“Zhang.”

It was the head guard, who normally didn’t come at this time. Zhang set down his book and stood up.

“The execution order has come down. Tomorrow morning at eight o’clock.”

The head guard asked:

“Is there anyone you’d like to see one last time?”

Zhang thought of Wei.

“No.”

“Will you write a will?”

“No.”

The head guard nodded and left.

Zhang sat back down and opened his book. It was harder to concentrate than before. He tried changing positions, but only the clanking of his chains became more grating to his ears.

He looked up toward the window. Beyond the iron bars, clouds were drifting in the wind. He realized that less than a day of life remained to him.

That evening, he polished his shackles with more care than usual.

The next morning at six o’clock, when the guards opened his cell door, Zhang was already awake.

“Get up.”

Zhang spoke the words he had been thinking:

“If time permits… please let me wash myself one last time.”

The guards exchanged glances and nodded. One guard removed Zhang’s handcuffs. Zhang went to the sink and washed his face and hands with cold water, then removed his shirt. He dampened a towel and methodically cleaned his neck, shoulders, armpits, and chest before putting his clothes back on.

“I’m ready.”

The guards handcuffed him again and pulled him from the cell. The chains dragged across the floor, echoing through the corridor.

When they reached the stairs, the guards expertly hooked their arms under his armpits and carried him down step by step. On the fifth step, the thick shackle ring caught on the edge of a stair. Zhang couldn’t suppress a groan as pain shot through his ankle like his foot was being severed. Cold sweat beaded on his forehead.

A guard asked:

“You alright?”

Zhang gritted his teeth and answered:

“Continue.”

The guards moved him even more carefully. It took fifteen minutes to descend to the first floor.

Zhang arrived at the waiting room. The small, windowless space contained one desk and one chair.

The guards seated Zhang in the chair and removed his handcuffs. One placed papers on the desk and pressed a pen into his hand.

“Sign this.”

It was an execution consent form. Zhang didn’t even read the text before writing his name immediately.

Another guard brought in a meal tray.

“Eat.”

It was a last meal prepared with more care than usual. White rice, stir-fried bok choy, braised tofu, a small fish. Zhang slowly chewed and swallowed each bite.

After the tray was cleared, a guard brought new clothes.

“Change into these.”

It was a pure white burial shroud. Zhang removed his prisoner uniform shirt and put on the traditional jacket first.

As Zhang sat in the chair, hunched over to thread the trouser legs through the gap between his shackle rings and ankles while removing his prison pants, one guard asked:

“Zhang, did you prepare a red envelope?”

“I don’t understand what you mean.”

The guard explained kindly:

“After we carry out the execution, we’ll remove your shackles. We have to break the rivets one by one with a hammer, and if the workers aren’t careful, they might hit your ankle bone. So when their time comes, everyone slips an envelope into their shackles. That way the workers are careful not to damage the bones when they use the hammer. The amount doesn’t need to be much. It’s the thought that counts. Ten to twenty coins are enough.”

“My entire estate was confiscated. I have no money.”

“Right… but I could contribute for you. Just write one sentence saying you’re leaving your shackles to me.”

Zhang, who had been gathering the waistband of his new trousers to pull them up through the shackle ring, tapped the metal ring with his finger.

“What would you use this for?”

“Usually other death row inmates would want to wear them. But yours are special six-kilogram custom pieces, so they can’t do that either.”

The guard pleaded earnestly:

“So give them to us. They’re blessed objects. Think of it as doing a good deed on your final journey.”

“A condemned man can do good deeds?”

One corner of Zhang’s mouth turned up.

“I’d prefer if you could take them right now, actually.”

“Hmm.”

The guard was serious. Zhang finally chuckled softly.

“The envelope isn’t necessary. Dispose of the shackles as you see fit.”

Zhang stood up, straightened his clothes, and put his arms behind his back. The guards took his arms, crossed them, and placed a rope around his neck. The rope pressed down on his shoulders, wound around his arms, circled his torso several times, and was knotted at his back. The five-flower binding was complete, and his upper body naturally tilted forward.

The guards led him outside the building and handed him over to soldiers. Four soldiers surrounded him. Two flanked him, each gripping one arm, while two positioned themselves behind him, each holding one shoulder.

The soldiers dragged him toward the execution ground. The chains clinked against the gravel. By now, this much didn’t even hurt.

Zhang smiled.

Only when I die will these shackles become truly blessed.

The gate to the execution ground opened. He would never know how the “Treatise on the Xiongnu” ended.

An hour later, Zhang’s corpse was transported to the workshop, shackles still attached.

“Red envelope?”

“None.”

“Hmm… well, that’ll make the work easier.”

And so Zhang was finally freed from his shackles.

The workshop foreman placed the six-kilogram shackles on the work table. Not a single rivet fragment was wasted.

First, they separated the ankle rings, locking mechanism, and chain. One ring went to the guard who had originally suggested that Zhang leave him the shackles.

“Wait, the warden said to save one ring. For ‘museum display,’ apparently.”

“What about the locking mechanism?”

“Let’s play rock-paper-scissors.”

The guards played rock-paper-scissors beside Zhang’s corpse. The winner triumphantly picked up the locking mechanism and pocketed it.

Next came the chain. They cut it with a hacksaw and disassembled it link by link. The foreman took one first, and the guards divided the rest among themselves.

Finally, the broken rivet fragments went to the inmates assigned to workshop cleaning.

“The body?”

“No one to claim it. Unclaimed disposal, scheduled for cremation.”

News of Zhang’s execution was reported nationwide as the fitting end of a corrupt official. But within Huaiyang Prefecture Correctional Facility, at least, the dead Zhang became the Illustrious Official once again. The facility warden whose repeat-exam son got into university, the guard whose wife’s illness inexplicably improved, the inmate selected for parole, the death row prisoner whose sentence was commuted to life by the Supreme Court—each in their own way honored the Illustrious Official’s blessed power. For them, the reasons Zhang had to die mattered less.
